Problem

Model predictive control re-plans a robot's motion many times per second, so the trajectory optimizer has to be fast. GPU solvers such as GATO are fast on desktop GPUs, but running one end to end on a real robot, on an embedded computer, was named as future work by the GATO and MPCGPU authors.

The question: can GATO control the 7-joint right arm of a TIAGo Pro mobile manipulator from the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin in the robot's backpack?

Approach

  • A reduced right-arm model derived from the robot description, with dynamics code generated by GRiD and a robot-specific plant wrapper and objective.
  • The MPC controller adapted to the robot's tasks and timing, in a closed loop through ROS 2, deployed on the Jetson.
  • A safety architecture for the real robot: self-collision monitoring with simplified geometry that hands over to the default position controller on a violation.
  • Evaluation in three settings: GATO's own simulation, Gazebo, and the physical arm; solve times compared on four GPUs.
  • Reproducible experiments: documented configurations, automated tests, diagnostics, and tools to record and replay runs.

pull requests merged into GATO
  • Viable for setpoint and trajectory tracking. With one SQP iteration, still more than 100 updates per second for horizons of several seconds.
  • In GATO's simulation, tracking on the Jetson is as accurate as on desktop GPUs up to 64 knot points. Gazebo and the real arm work end to end, but with degraded performance that points to the robot model, not to the solver.
  • Force estimation: on the embedded GPU a batch takes several times as long as one problem, so I batch only the dynamics instead of whole optimisation problems. Same compensation of an external force, 13.0 instead of 44.9 ms per update.
  • What didn't work: batches of initial guesses (multi-start) found better motions in tuned experiments, e.g. around a moving obstacle, but didn't reach more goals in general.
  • Open source: porting exposed defects in GATO. Merged fixes include an ignored terminal cost weight (#17), a read/write race in the Schur assembly that also made batched solves about 35% faster (#18), and a crash on arms with mixed joint axes (#21).
